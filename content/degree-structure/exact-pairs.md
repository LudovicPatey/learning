summary: The Turing degrees form an upper semilattice with countable lower cones and no maximal element; every countable ideal has an exact pair; consequently the degrees do not form a lattice, and strictly increasing sequences have no least upper bound.
duration: 50

The Turing degrees $\mathcal D$, ordered by Turing reducibility, form a partial order whose study began with Kleene and Post (1954). This course examines its global structure: which bounds exist, which partial orders appear inside it, and whether it has minimal elements above $\obf$. This first lesson gathers the elementary properties of $\mathcal D$ and proves Spector's exact pair theorem, which shows that the degrees are far from forming a lattice. The proof is a finite extension construction (:ref{to="turing-degrees/finite-extensions"}) in which the strings are only allowed to grow in a restricted way.

For a set $G$ and $k \in \nat$, the **column** $G^{[k]}$ is the set $\{ y : \langle k, y \rangle \in G \}$. For sets $A_0, A_1, \dots$, the set $\bigoplus_n A_n$ has columns $A_n$.

## Elementary properties

:::proposition{#prop-degrees-elementary}
1. $\obf$ is the least degree, and every degree has at most countably many degrees below it.
2. Every degree has continuum many degrees above it, and no degree is maximal.
3. Every two degrees have a least upper bound, the degree of the join: $\mathcal D$ is an upper semilattice.
4. Every countable set of degrees has an upper bound.
:::

:::proof{of=prop-degrees-elementary}
1. Computable sets are computable from every set; lower cones are countable by :ref{#prop-degree-countable}.
2. Let $A$ be a set. The sets $A \oplus X$, $X \in \cs$, compute $A$, and they are continuum many. Each degree contains only countably many sets, so they fall into continuum many degrees. Since only one of them is $\deg_T(A)$ itself, $\deg_T(A)$ is not maximal; an explicit degree strictly above it is $\deg_T(A')$ (:ref{#thm-jump}).
3. This is :ref{#prop-join-lub}.
4. Given representatives $X_n$, the set $\bigoplus_n X_n$ computes each of them.
:::

An upper bound of a countable set of degrees given by (4) is not a least upper bound in general. The rest of the lesson shows that a strictly increasing sequence never has one. The asymmetry between (1) and (2) already shows that $\mathcal D$ is not isomorphic to its reverse order: lower cones are countable, upper cones are not.

## Ideals and exact pairs

A non-empty set $\mathcal I$ of degrees is an **ideal** if it is closed downward and under $\vee$. The countable ideals are exactly the sets of the form
$$
\mathcal I(A_0, A_1, \dots) = \{ \deg_T(X) : X \leq_T A_0 \oplus \cdots \oplus A_n \text{ for some } n \},
$$
the ideal **generated** by a sequence of sets: such a set is an ideal, and a countable ideal is generated by any sequence of representatives of its elements.

:::example{#ex-ideals}
1. For a degree $\abf$, the lower cone $\{ \cbf : \cbf \leq \abf \}$ is an ideal, the **principal** ideal of $\abf$. It is generated by the constant sequence $A, A, \dots$ with $A \in \abf$, and $\{ \obf \}$ is the principal ideal of $\obf$.
2. The **arithmetic degrees**, the degrees below $\obf^{(n)}$ for some $n$, form the ideal generated by $\emptyset, \emptyset', \emptyset'', \dots$, since $\emptyset^{(0)} \oplus \cdots \oplus \emptyset^{(n)} \equiv_T \emptyset^{(n)}$. It is not principal: a greatest element $\abf$ would satisfy $\abf \leq \obf^{(n)}$ for some $n$, and $\obf^{(n+1)} \leq \abf$, contradicting $\obf^{(n)} < \obf^{(n+1)}$.
3. If $\abf$ and $\bbf$ are incomparable, the union of their lower cones is closed downward but not under $\vee$: the degree $\abf \vee \bbf$ is not below $\abf$, since otherwise $\bbf \leq \abf$, and similarly not below $\bbf$.
:::

A countable ideal is principal if and only if it has a greatest element. A principal ideal is described by a single degree. An ideal without greatest element cannot be: if $\mathcal I = \{ \cbf : \cbf \leq \abf \}$, then $\abf \in \mathcal I$ is its greatest element. The next definition describes such an ideal by two degrees.

:::definition{#def-exact-pair}
Two degrees $\abf$ and $\bbf$ form an **exact pair** for an ideal $\mathcal I$ if $\mathcal I = \{ \cbf : \cbf \leq \abf \text{ and } \cbf \leq \bbf \}$.
:::

So $\abf$ and $\bbf$ are upper bounds of $\mathcal I$, and every common lower bound of $\abf$ and $\bbf$ belongs to $\mathcal I$. A principal ideal of $\abf$ has the exact pair $\abf, \abf$; the content of the next theorem is in the ideals without greatest element.

:::theorem[Spector]{#thm-exact-pair}
Every countable ideal of Turing degrees has an exact pair.
:::

*Idea of the proof.* Let $\mathcal I$ be generated by $A_0, A_1, \dots$. We build $G$ and $H$ that both contain a copy of every $A_k$, up to finitely many bits, in their column $k$: this makes them upper bounds. Then we must prevent a set from being computed by both $G$ and $H$ unless it lies in $\mathcal I$. A requirement concerns a pair of reductions $\Phi_i^G$ and $\Phi_j^H$. The construction first tries to make them disagree, by extending $G$ and $H$ to strings on which they give different values. If this is impossible, all extensions give compatible values, so a common value can be found by searching among the extensions. The obstacle is that $G$ and $H$ are not free: from some stage on, their columns $k < s$ must copy $A_k$, and these columns are said to be **locked**. So the search runs over the extensions that respect the locked columns, and recognizing them requires the oracle $A_0 \oplus \cdots \oplus A_{s-1}$, which lies in $\mathcal I$. This is the finite extension method of :ref{#thm-kleene-post}, with conditions restricted by the locked columns.

:::proof{of=thm-exact-pair}
Let $\mathcal I$ be generated by $A_0, A_1, \dots$, and let $B_s = A_0 \oplus \cdots \oplus A_{s-1}$, with $B_0 = \emptyset$. We build $G = \bigcup_s g_s$ and $H = \bigcup_s h_s$ as limits of strings, so that the column $k$ of $G$ and of $H$ agrees with $A_k$ from some point on.

For strings $\sigma \preceq \tau$, say that $\tau$ is an **$s$-admissible** extension of $\sigma$ if $\tau(\langle k, y \rangle) = A_k(y)$ for every position $\langle k, y \rangle$ with $k < s$ and $|\sigma| \leq \langle k, y \rangle < |\tau|$. Columns $k < s$ are **locked** beyond $\sigma$: an admissible extension must copy $A_k$ there. Admissibility of $\tau$ over $\sigma$ is decidable with oracle $B_s$, uniformly in $s$.

Fix an enumeration $s \mapsto (i_s, j_s)$ of all pairs of indices. Start with $g_0 = h_0 = \varepsilon$. At stage $s$:

1. If there are $x$ and $s$-admissible extensions $g'$ of $g_s$ and $h'$ of $h_s$ with $\Phi^{g'}_{i_s}(x)\converge \neq \Phi^{h'}_{j_s}(x)\converge$, take such $g', h'$. Otherwise let $g' = g_s$ and $h' = h_s$.
2. Extend $g'$ and $h'$ by one bit to obtain $g_{s+1}$ and $h_{s+1}$: at a position $p = \langle k, y \rangle$, the new bit is $A_k(y)$ if $k \leq s$, and $0$ otherwise.

So $g_{s+1}$ is an $s$-admissible extension of $g_s$, and $h_{s+1}$ of $h_s$. Since $t$-admissibility implies $s$-admissibility for $t \geq s$, every $G \uh m$ with $m \geq |g_s|$ is an $s$-admissible extension of $g_s$, and similarly for $H$.

*$\abf = \deg_T(G)$ and $\bbf = \deg_T(H)$ are upper bounds of $\mathcal I$.* Fix $k$. At every stage $s \geq k + 1$, the new positions of $G$ in column $k$ receive the values of $A_k$, since all extensions made at stage $s$ are $s$-admissible and the new bit follows $A_k$. So $G(\langle k, y \rangle) = A_k(y)$ for all positions $\langle k, y \rangle \geq |g_{k+1}|$. Hence $A_k$ is a finite variant of $G^{[k]}$, and $A_k \leq_T G$. The same holds for $H$.

*Common lower bounds belong to $\mathcal I$.* Let $Y \leq_T G$ and $Y \leq_T H$, say $Y = \Phi^G_i = \Phi^H_j$, and let $s$ be the stage with $(i_s, j_s) = (i, j)$. At stage $s$, no pair of admissible extensions with distinct convergent values was found: otherwise $G \succeq g'$ and $H \succeq h'$ would give $\Phi^G_i(x) \neq \Phi^H_j(x)$. We compute $Y$ with oracle $B_s$. Given $x$, search for an $s$-admissible extension $g'$ of $g_s$ with $\Phi^{g'}_i(x)\converge$, and output the value $v$.

- The search terminates: $G \uh m$ is an $s$-admissible extension of $g_s$ for every $m \geq |g_s|$, and $\Phi^{G \uh m}_i(x)\converge$ for large $m$.
- The output is $Y(x)$: similarly, $H \uh m$ is an $s$-admissible extension of $h_s$ with $\Phi^{H \uh m}_j(x)\converge = Y(x)$ for large $m$. Since no admissible pair gives distinct values, $v = Y(x)$.

So $Y \leq_T B_s$, and $\deg_T(Y) \in \mathcal I$.
:::

Each requirement is handled once, at a single stage: either it is satisfied by a disagreement, or the absence of any disagreement among admissible extensions gives a procedure computing the common value from the locked columns alone. The locking of columns makes $G$ and $H$ upper bounds; it also limits the freedom of later extensions, which is why the procedure needs the oracle $B_s$. Two sets are needed: the search computing $Y$ compares the extensions of $g_s$ with the actual set $H$, and a single upper bound $G$ of an ideal without greatest element always computes sets outside the ideal, for instance $G$ itself.

:::quiz[the exact pair theorem]
In the proof of :ref{#thm-exact-pair}, why does the procedure computing $Y$ need the oracle $B_s$?

- [x] To recognize the $s$-admissible extensions of $g_s$, which must copy $A_0, \dots, A_{s-1}$ on the locked columns.
- [ ] To decide whether $\Phi^{g'}_i(x)$ converges.

  Convergence is found by a search; the oracle is needed to decide which strings may be searched.
- [ ] Because $Y$ is equal to $B_s$.

  $Y$ is computable from $B_s$, and may be much simpler.

---

Why is the value found by the search equal to $Y(x)$, even though $g'$ may not be a prefix of $G$?

- [x] Because $H \uh m$ is an admissible extension of $h_s$ computing $Y(x)$, and no admissible pair gives distinct convergent values: any admissible $g'$ with a convergent value agrees with $Y(x)$.
- [ ] Because $\Phi_i$ is total on every oracle.

  $\Phi_i$ may be partial; the argument only uses convergent computations.
- [ ] Because all admissible extensions of $g_s$ are prefixes of $G$.

  Most of them are not; the argument compares them with $H$.

---

Why must the search range over the admissible extensions of $g_s$ only, rather than over all its extensions, which would make the procedure computable?

- [x] Because the construction only excluded disagreements between admissible extensions: an arbitrary extension of $g_s$ may give a value different from $Y(x)$.
- [ ] Because arbitrary extensions of $g_s$ never make $\Phi_i$ converge.

  They may well make it converge, possibly with a wrong value; that is the problem.
- [ ] Because $Y$ is never computable.

  $Y$ may be computable; the theorem only asserts $Y \leq_T B_s$.
:::

## Consequences

:::corollary[Kleene, Post; Spector]{#cor-not-lattice}
1. A strictly increasing sequence $\dbf_0 < \dbf_1 < \cdots$ of degrees has no least upper bound.
2. $\mathcal D$ is not a lattice: there are two degrees without a greatest lower bound.
:::

:::proof{of=cor-not-lattice}
Let $D_n \in \dbf_n$, and let $\abf, \bbf$ be an exact pair for the ideal generated by $(D_n)$. Since the sequence increases, $D_0 \oplus \cdots \oplus D_n \equiv_T D_n$, so this ideal is the set of degrees $\cbf$ such that $\cbf \leq \dbf_n$ for some $n$.

1. Suppose $\mathbf l$ is a least upper bound of the sequence. The degrees $\abf$ and $\bbf$ are upper bounds of the sequence, so $\mathbf l \leq \abf$ and $\mathbf l \leq \bbf$. Hence $\mathbf l$ is in the ideal: $\mathbf l \leq \dbf_n$ for some $n$. But $\dbf_{n+1} \leq \mathbf l$, so $\dbf_{n+1} \leq \dbf_n$, a contradiction.
2. Every common lower bound $\cbf$ of $\abf$ and $\bbf$ is below some $\dbf_n$, hence strictly below $\dbf_{n+1}$, which is another common lower bound. So no common lower bound is the greatest.
:::

The hypothesis that the sequence increases strictly is needed in (1): an eventually constant sequence has the least upper bound $\dbf_n$ for $n$ large. For instance, the arithmetic degrees (:ref{#ex-ideals}) are exactly the common lower bounds of some pair of degrees, although they have no least upper bound. Some pairs of degrees do have a greatest lower bound: comparable degrees, and the minimal pairs of the course on infinite injury, whose meet is $\obf$. Instead of a least upper bound, an increasing sequence has **minimal upper bounds**; they are built with the trees of the last two lessons (:ref{to="degree-structure/minimal-covers"}).

Exact pairs are a basic tool for coding countable structures into $\mathcal D$. They are used in the proof that the first-order theory of $\mathcal D$ is as complex as true second-order arithmetic (Simpson, 1977), and in the definability results of Slaman and Woodin.

## Exercises

::::exercise{difficulty=1}
Show that the arithmetic degrees, the degrees below $\obf^{(n)}$ for some $n$, have no least upper bound, and that there are two degrees whose common lower bounds are exactly the arithmetic degrees.

:::solution
The sequence $\obf^{(n)}$ is strictly increasing (:ref{#thm-jump}), so :ref{#cor-not-lattice} (1) applies. The arithmetic degrees form the ideal generated by $(\emptyset^{(n)})_n$ (:ref{#ex-ideals}). Apply :ref{#thm-exact-pair}.
:::
::::

::::exercise{difficulty=2}
Show that in :ref{#thm-exact-pair}, the sets $G$ and $H$ can be chosen computable in $\big(\bigoplus_n A_n\big)'$.

:::solution
Let $B = \bigoplus_n A_n$; it computes each $B_s$ uniformly in $s$. The question asked at stage $s$, whether there exist $x$ and $s$-admissible extensions with distinct convergent values, is $\Sigma^0_1$ relative to $B_s$, uniformly in $s$, $g_s$ and $h_s$, hence decided by $B'$. When the answer is positive, the witnesses are found by a $B$-computable search. The extension by one bit uses $A_k(y)$ for $k \leq s$, computable in $B$. So the sequences $(g_s)$ and $(h_s)$ are computable in $B'$.
:::
::::

::::exercise{difficulty=2}
Let $\mathcal I$ be a countable ideal and $\abf$ an upper bound of $\mathcal I$. Show that there is a degree $\bbf$ such that $\abf, \bbf$ is an exact pair for $\mathcal I$.

:::solution
Let $G \in \abf$, let $A_0, A_1, \dots$ generate $\mathcal I$, and build only $H = \bigcup_s h_s$, with the admissible extensions of the proof of :ref{#thm-exact-pair}. At stage $s$, if there are $x$ and an $s$-admissible extension $h'$ of $h_s$ with $\Phi^G_{i_s}(x)\converge \neq \Phi^{h'}_{j_s}(x)\converge$, take such an $h'$; otherwise let $h' = h_s$. Then extend $h'$ by one bit as in the proof. As before, $H$ is an upper bound of $\mathcal I$, and so is $G$ by hypothesis. Let $Y = \Phi^G_i = \Phi^H_j$, and $s$ the stage with $(i_s, j_s) = (i, j)$. If a disagreement had been found at stage $s$, then $H \succeq h'$ would give $\Phi^H_j(x) \neq \Phi^G_i(x)$; so none was found. To compute $Y(x)$ with oracle $B_s$, search for an $s$-admissible extension $h'$ of $h_s$ with $\Phi^{h'}_j(x)\converge$, and output its value. The search terminates, since long prefixes of $H$ qualify. Since $\Phi^G_i(x)$ converges to $Y(x)$ and no admissible extension disagrees with it, the output is $Y(x)$. So $Y \leq_T B_s$. The set $G$ is only used to decide the case at each stage, never in the computation of $Y$. This is Soare's Exercise 6.5.11.
:::
::::

## Notes and references

Kleene and Post (1954) founded the study of the degrees as a partial order; Downey and Hirschfeldt (Section 2.4) credit them with the fact that $\mathcal D$ is not a lattice. Spector (1956) proved the exact pair theorem for ascending sequences, in the paper that also constructs a minimal degree. Soare's *Turing Computability* (2016) proves the theorem for ascending sequences as Theorem 6.5.3, which he names after Kleene, Post and Spector, with Corollaries 6.5.4 and 6.5.5 for the two consequences above; the version for ideals is his Exercise 6.5.12, and the bound of the second exercise is his Exercise 6.5.10. Monin and Patey prove the theorem with a forcing whose conditions are pairs of strings with a number of locked columns, in their chapter on the structure of the degrees. Lerman's monograph is the standard reference on the global structure of $\mathcal D$.
