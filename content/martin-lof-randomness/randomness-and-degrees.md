Random sequences are typical with respect to measure, and one may ask what they can compute. Two results point in opposite directions. Sacks's theorem says that a non-computable set is computed by a null class of oracles only: a sequence chosen at random computes it with probability $0$. The Kučera–Gács theorem says that every set is nevertheless computed by some Martin-Löf random sequence. The lesson ends with a computational property shared by all random sequences: they compute diagonally non-computable functions.

For a string $\tau$, $\Phi^\tau_e(n)\converge = i$ means that the computation of $\Phi_e$ on input $n$ with oracle $\tau$ halts with output $i$ and only queries the oracle below $|\tau|$; by :ref{#prop-oracle-stages}, it then holds with every oracle extending $\tau$.

## Upper cones are null

:::theorem[Sacks]{#thm-sacks}
If $Y$ is not computable, then $\lambda(\{ X : Y \leq_T X \}) = 0$.
:::

:::proof{of=thm-sacks}
The class $\{ X : Y \leq_T X \}$ is the union over $e$ of the classes $\mathcal C_e = \{ X : \Phi^X_e = Y \}$. Each $\mathcal C_e$ is Borel: $X \in \mathcal C_e$ if and only if for every $n$ there is a prefix $\tau$ of $X$ with $\Phi^\tau_e(n)\converge = Y(n)$. By subadditivity, it suffices to show that each $\mathcal C_e$ is null.

Suppose that $\lambda(\mathcal C_e) > 0$. By the density lemma (:ref{#lem-density}), there is a string $\sigma$ with $\lambda(\mathcal C_e \mid \sigma) > \frac12$. For $n \in \nat$ and $i \in \{0, 1\}$, let $\mathcal U_{n,i}$ be the open class generated by the strings $\tau \succeq \sigma$ with $\Phi^\tau_e(n)\converge = i$; these classes are uniformly $\Sigma^0_1$.

- $\mathcal C_e \cap [\sigma] \subseteq \mathcal U_{n, Y(n)}$, so $\lambda(\mathcal U_{n, Y(n)} \mid \sigma) > \frac12$.
- $\mathcal U_{n,0}$ and $\mathcal U_{n,1}$ are disjoint: a sequence in both would have prefixes $\tau, \tau'$ with $\Phi^\tau_e(n)\converge = 0$ and $\Phi^{\tau'}_e(n)\converge = 1$, and by monotonicity the longer of the two would give both outputs. So $\lambda(\mathcal U_{n, 1 - Y(n)} \mid \sigma) < \frac12$.

To compute $Y(n)$, enumerate $\mathcal U_{n,0}$ and $\mathcal U_{n,1}$ until the measure of the finite part of one of them inside $[\sigma]$ exceeds $2^{-|\sigma| - 1}$, and output the corresponding $i$. The search terminates by the first item, since the measure of $\mathcal U_{n, Y(n)} \cap [\sigma]$ is approached from below, and its output is $Y(n)$ by the second. This computes $Y$, a contradiction.
:::

The proof is a majority vote: if a positive proportion of the oracles compute $Y$ through $\Phi_e$, the oracles extending $\sigma$ agree in their majority with $Y$, and the majority can be detected effectively. Sacks's theorem is the measure-theoretic analogue of the fact that upper cones of non-computable sets are meager.

:::quiz[Sacks's theorem]
In the proof of :ref{#thm-sacks}, why is the density lemma needed, rather than the hypothesis $\lambda(\mathcal C_e) > 0$ alone?

- [x] Because the vote must be won by a majority: on a cylinder where $\mathcal C_e$ has relative measure above $\frac12$, the correct value has more than half of the measure and the incorrect one less than half.
- [ ] Because $\mathcal C_e$ might not be measurable.

  It is Borel, as shown at the beginning of the proof.
- [ ] Because the density lemma makes $\mathcal C_e$ open.

  $\mathcal C_e$ is not open in general; the lemma only finds a cylinder where it is dense.

---

Why are the classes $\mathcal U_{n,0}$ and $\mathcal U_{n,1}$ disjoint?

- [x] Because a computation with an oracle string is preserved by extending the string, so two prefixes of the same sequence cannot produce different outputs on the same input.
- [ ] Because $Y(n)$ has only one value.

  The classes are defined without reference to $Y$; disjointness comes from the oracle machine.
- [ ] Because $\Phi_e$ is total.

  $\Phi^X_e$ may be partial for many $X$; disjointness concerns the oracles on which it converges.
:::

:::corollary{#cor-random-cone}
For every non-computable $Y$, almost every sequence fails to compute $Y$. In particular the class of sequences computing $\emptyset'$ is null.
:::

This class nevertheless contains random sequences, such as $\Omega$ (:ref{#cor-omega-random}). Martin-Löf tests capture only effectively null classes, and the null class $\{ X : \emptyset' \leq_T X \}$, which is $\Sigma^0_4$, is not contained in any of them.

## Every set is computable from a random sequence

A computable tree in which every node has two incomparable extensions has members of every Turing degree above its own. The tree of a $\Pi^0_1$ class may have dead ends, and one cannot tell effectively which nodes branch. When the class has positive measure, branching nodes appear at a bounded depth.

:::lemma[Kučera]{#lem-kucera-branching}
Let $\mathcal B$ be a Borel class, $\sigma$ a string and $k \in \nat$ with $\lambda(\mathcal B \mid \sigma) \geq 2^{-k}$. Then there are at least two strings $\tau \succeq \sigma$ of length $|\sigma| + k + 1$ with $\lambda(\mathcal B \mid \tau) \geq 2^{-k-1}$.
:::

:::proof{of=lem-kucera-branching}
$\lambda(\mathcal B \mid \sigma)$ is the average of $\lambda(\mathcal B \mid \tau)$ over the $2^{k+1}$ strings $\tau \succeq \sigma$ of length $|\sigma| + k + 1$. If at most one of them had relative measure at least $2^{-k-1}$, this one being at most $1$ and the others less than $2^{-k-1}$, the average would be less than
$$
2^{-k-1} \left( 1 + (2^{k+1} - 1)\, 2^{-k-1} \right) = 2^{-k-1} + 2^{-k-1} - 2^{-2k-2} < 2^{-k},
$$
a contradiction.
:::

:::theorem[Kučera, Gács]{#thm-kucera-gacs}
Let $\mathcal P$ be a $\Pi^0_1$ class of positive measure. For every set $X$, there is $Z \in \mathcal P$ with $X \leq_T Z$ and $Z \leq_T X \oplus \emptyset'$. In particular, every set is computable from a Martin-Löf random sequence, and every Turing degree above $\obf'$ contains a random sequence.
:::

:::proof{of=thm-kucera-gacs}
Let $\mathcal P = [T]$ with $T$ computable, and let $c$ be such that $\lambda(\mathcal P) \geq 2^{-c}$. Let $m_0 = 0$ and $m_{n+1} = m_n + c + n + 1$. For a string $\sigma$ of length $m_n$, let $A_n(\sigma)$ be the set of strings $\tau \succeq \sigma$ of length $m_{n+1}$ with $\lambda(\mathcal P \mid \tau) \geq 2^{-c-n-1}$. By :ref{#lem-kucera-branching} with $k = c + n$, if $\lambda(\mathcal P \mid \sigma) \geq 2^{-c-n}$, then $A_n(\sigma)$ has at least two elements.

*Construction.* Let $\sigma_0$ be the empty string; $\lambda(\mathcal P \mid \sigma_0) = \lambda(\mathcal P) \geq 2^{-c}$. Given $\sigma_n$ of length $m_n$ with $\lambda(\mathcal P \mid \sigma_n) \geq 2^{-c-n}$, let $\sigma_{n+1}$ be the leftmost element of $A_n(\sigma_n)$ if $X(n) = 0$, and the rightmost one if $X(n) = 1$, for the lexicographic order. It satisfies $\lambda(\mathcal P \mid \sigma_{n+1}) \geq 2^{-c-n-1}$. Let $Z = \bigcup_n \sigma_n$. Each $[\sigma_n]$ meets $\mathcal P$, since it has positive relative measure, so every prefix of $Z$ is extendible in $T$ and $Z \in \mathcal P$.

*$Z \leq_T X \oplus \emptyset'$.* By :ref{#prop-measure-effective} (2) applied to the $\Pi^0_1$ class $\mathcal P \cap [\tau]$, the relative measure $\lambda(\mathcal P \mid \tau)$ is right-c.e., uniformly in $\tau$. So the condition $\lambda(\mathcal P \mid \tau) \geq q$, for a rational $q$, is $\Pi^0_1$: it fails if and only if some approximation from above falls below $q$. With $\emptyset'$, one computes $A_n(\sigma_n)$ from $\sigma_n$, and with $X$ one chooses $\sigma_{n+1}$.

*$X \leq_T Z$.* The sets $A_n(\sigma)$ are co-c.e. uniformly in $n$ and $\sigma$: there are finite sets $A_{n,s}(\sigma)$, computable from $n$, $\sigma$, $s$, decreasing in $s$, with intersection $A_n(\sigma)$. To compute $X(n)$, let $\sigma = Z \uh m_n = \sigma_n$ and $\tau = Z \uh m_{n+1} = \sigma_{n+1}$, and search for a stage $s$ at which $\tau$ is the leftmost or the rightmost element of $A_{n,s}(\sigma)$; output $0$ in the first case and $1$ in the second. Suppose $X(n) = 0$, so that $\tau$ is the leftmost element of $A_n(\sigma)$. The finitely many strings of length $m_{n+1}$ to the left of $\tau$ are not in $A_n(\sigma)$, so they leave $A_{n,s}(\sigma)$ at some stage, and $\tau$ becomes leftmost. On the other hand, $A_n(\sigma)$ has an element to the right of $\tau$, which belongs to every $A_{n,s}(\sigma)$, so $\tau$ is never the rightmost element. The search therefore returns $0$. The case $X(n) = 1$ is symmetric.

For the last statements, take for $\mathcal P$ the $\Pi^0_1$ class of random sequences of :ref{#cor-mlr-structure} (2), of measure at least $\frac12$. If $X \geq_T \emptyset'$, then $Z \leq_T X \oplus \emptyset' \equiv_T X$, so $Z \equiv_T X$.
:::

The reduction of $X$ to $Z$ reads only $Z \uh m_{n+1}$ to compute $X(n)$, a computable bound on the use: it is a **weak truth-table** reduction. The encoding is not computable, since recognizing the nodes $\tau$ with large relative measure requires $\emptyset'$, but the decoding is: the decoder waits for the competitors of $\tau$ to reveal that they have small relative measure.

:::quiz[the Kučera–Gács theorem]
In the decoding procedure of :ref{#thm-kucera-gacs}, why can the search not return the wrong bit?

- [x] Because $A_n(\sigma)$ has at least two elements: if $\tau$ is its leftmost element, another element lies to its right and stays in every approximation, so $\tau$ never appears as the rightmost one.
- [ ] Because the approximations $A_{n,s}(\sigma)$ are computable.

  Computability makes the search effective; correctness comes from the second element of $A_n(\sigma)$.
- [ ] Because $\tau$ belongs to $\mathcal P$.

  $\tau$ is a string; what matters is its position among the strings of $A_n(\sigma)$.

---

Why are the lengths chosen with $m_{n+1} - m_n = c + n + 1$, growing with $n$?

- [x] Because the relative measure guaranteed for $\sigma_n$ is only $2^{-c-n}$, halved at each step, and :ref{#lem-kucera-branching} finds two branching extensions at depth $c + n + 1$ for such a relative measure.
- [ ] Because $X(n)$ must be encoded with $n$ bits.

  Each bit of $X$ is encoded by a binary choice; the extra length is the price of finding a branching.
- [ ] To make $Z$ random.

  $Z$ is random because it lies in $\mathcal P$, whatever the lengths.
:::

## Random sequences compute DNC functions

Recall from :ref{#def-dnc} that a function $f$ is **diagonally non-computable** if $f(e) \neq \Phi_e(e)$ for every $e$ with $\Phi_e(e)\converge$. Strings are identified with numbers through the length-lexicographic enumeration of :ref{to="kolmogorov-complexity/plain-complexity"}.

:::theorem[Kjos-Hanssen, Merkle, Stephan]{#thm-dnc-complexity}
A set $X$ computes a DNC function if and only if $X$ computes a function $f : \nat \to \bstr$ with $K(f(n)) \geq n$ for every $n$.
:::

:::proof{of=thm-dnc-complexity}
($\Rightarrow$) Let $h \leq_T X$ be as in :ref{#thm-dnc-effectively-immune} (2): $h(e, m) \notin W_e$ whenever $|W_e| \leq m$. Since $K$ is approximable from above, the set of strings $\sigma$ with $K(\sigma) < n$ is c.e. uniformly in $n$, and by SMN there is a total computable function $a$ such that $W_{a(n)}$ is this set. It has fewer than $2^n$ elements, since distinct strings have distinct shortest descriptions, of length less than $n$. Let $f(n) = h(a(n), 2^n)$. Then $f \leq_T X$ and $f(n) \notin W_{a(n)}$, that is, $K(f(n)) \geq n$.

($\Leftarrow$) Let $f \leq_T X$ with $K(f(n)) \geq n$ for every $n$. The machine $M(\tau) = \Phi_{U(\tau)}(U(\tau))$ is prefix-free, since its domain is contained in that of $U$, and $M$ applied to a shortest description of $n$ gives $\Phi_n(n)$ when it converges. So there is a constant $d$ with
$$
K(\Phi_n(n)) \leq K(n) + d \leq 2 \log n + d'
$$
for every $n \geq 2$ with $\Phi_n(n)\converge$, by :ref{#prop-k-basic} (2). Let $n_0 \geq 2$ be such that $2 \log n + d' < n$ for $n \geq n_0$. For $n \geq n_0$ with $\Phi_n(n)\converge$, $K(\Phi_n(n)) < n \leq K(f(n))$, so $f(n) \neq \Phi_n(n)$. Modify $f$ on the finitely many $n < n_0$, setting $g(n) = \Phi_n(n) + 1$ if $\Phi_n(n)\converge$ and $g(n) = 0$ otherwise, and $g(n) = f(n)$ for $n \geq n_0$. The function $g$ is $X$-computable, as a finite modification of $f$, and DNC.
:::

:::corollary[Kučera]{#cor-random-dnc}
Every Martin-Löf random sequence computes a DNC function. Consequently, the class of sequences computing a DNC function is conull, and no 1-generic set computes a Martin-Löf random sequence.
:::

:::proof{of=cor-random-dnc}
Let $Z$ be random. By :ref{#thm-levin-schnorr}, there is $c$ with $K(Z \uh m) \geq m - c$ for every $m$. The function $f(n) = Z \uh (n + c)$ is $Z$-computable and satisfies $K(f(n)) \geq n$, so $Z$ computes a DNC function by :ref{#thm-dnc-complexity}. The class MLR is conull (:ref{#cor-mlr-structure}). A 1-generic set computes no DNC function, by a theorem of Demuth and Kučera proved in the course on Cohen genericity (:ref{#thm-generic-not-dnc}); hence it computes no random sequence.
:::

The two notions of typicality, by measure and by category, are thus incompatible in the Turing degrees: no random sequence is computable from a 1-generic set. By Kučera–Gács, every 1-generic set is computable from a random sequence.

:::quiz[randomness and DNC functions]
In the proof of :ref{#thm-dnc-complexity} ($\Leftarrow$), why is $f(n) \neq \Phi_n(n)$ for large $n$?

- [x] Because the value $\Phi_n(n)$, when defined, is computed from $n$ and has complexity at most about $2 \log n$, whereas $f(n)$ has complexity at least $n$.
- [ ] Because $f$ is not computable.

  A non-computable function may agree with $\Phi_n(n)$ for infinitely many $n$; the argument uses the complexity bound.
- [ ] Because $K(n) \geq n$.

  On the contrary, $K(n) \leq^+ 2 \log n$ is much smaller than $n$.

---

In the proof of :ref{#cor-random-dnc}, why is $Z \uh (n + c)$ used rather than $Z \uh n$?

- [x] To compensate the constant $c$ of the Levin–Schnorr theorem: $K(Z \uh (n + c)) \geq n$, as required by :ref{#thm-dnc-complexity}.
- [ ] Because $Z \uh n$ is not $Z$-computable.

  Both are $Z$-computable; the shift adjusts the complexity bound.
- [ ] Because $c$ must be computable from $Z$.

  $c$ is a fixed constant; no computation of it is needed.
:::

The converse fails: there are DNC functions that compute no random sequence (Ambos-Spies, Kjos-Hanssen, Lempp, Slaman, 2004). A set computes a DNC function if and only if it computes an infinite subset of a random sequence, by results of Kjos-Hanssen (2009) and of Greenberg and Miller (2009); this is admitted here. A random sequence computes a $\{0, 1\}$-valued DNC function only if it computes $\emptyset'$, by a theorem of Stephan (2006), also admitted.

## Exercises

::::exercise{difficulty=1}
Show that if a random sequence $Z$ is Turing equivalent to a c.e. set $A$, then $Z \equiv_T \emptyset'$.

:::solution
By :ref{#cor-random-dnc}, $Z$ computes a DNC function, hence so does $A$. By Arslanov's completeness criterion (:ref{#thm-arslanov}), $A \equiv_T \emptyset'$.
:::
::::

::::exercise{difficulty=1}
Show that for every $n \geq 1$ there is a random sequence $Z \equiv_T \emptyset^{(n)}$, and that the class of such sequences is null.

:::solution
Apply :ref{#thm-kucera-gacs} to $X = \emptyset^{(n)}$, which computes $\emptyset'$: the random sequence $Z$ obtained satisfies $Z \equiv_T \emptyset^{(n)}$. The class of sequences computing $\emptyset^{(n)}$ is null by :ref{#thm-sacks}, and contains the class considered.
:::
::::

::::exercise{difficulty=1}
Let $\mathcal P$ be a $\Pi^0_1$ class of positive measure. Show that $\mathcal P$ has a member in every Turing degree above $\obf'$, and a member that is not computably dominated. Compare with the computably dominated basis theorem.

:::solution
For $X \geq_T \emptyset'$, :ref{#thm-kucera-gacs} gives $Z \in \mathcal P$ with $X \leq_T Z \leq_T X \oplus \emptyset' \equiv_T X$. With $X = \emptyset'$, the member $Z \equiv_T \emptyset'$ is $\Delta^0_2$ and not computable, hence not computably dominated (:ref{#def-computably-dominated}). By :ref{#thm-dominated-basis}, $\mathcal P$ also has a computably dominated member: a $\Pi^0_1$ class of positive measure has members of both kinds.
:::
::::

## Notes and references

Sacks's theorem is from his monograph *Degrees of Unsolvability* (1963); de Leeuw, Moore, Shannon and Shapiro (1956) had proved that a set computed by a positive measure of oracles is computable, in the form of a theorem on probabilistic machines. The Kučera–Gács theorem was proved independently by Kučera (1985) and Gács (1986); Gács obtained a use bounded by $n + o(n)$, and Barmpalias and Lewis-Pye studied the optimal use. Kučera (1985) proved that random sequences compute DNC functions, and Kjos-Hanssen, Merkle and Stephan (2011) the characterization by complexity.

- P. Gács, *Every sequence is reducible to a random one*, Information and Control 70 (1986), 186–192.
- B. Kjos-Hanssen, W. Merkle and F. Stephan, *Kolmogorov complexity and the recursion theorem*, Transactions of the American Mathematical Society 363 (2011), 5465–5480.
