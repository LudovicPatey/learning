The previous courses gave two descriptions of random sequences: their initial segments cannot be compressed, and they avoid every effectively null class. The third description is by **unpredictability**: no betting strategy makes unbounded profit when it bets on the successive bits of a random sequence. This course develops it. The first two lessons contain no effectivity. This one formalizes betting strategies as martingales and proves the inequality that controls them, Kolmogorov's inequality; the next one shows, following Ville (1939), that the classes on which a martingale wins are exactly the null classes. The later lessons restrict the strategies to effective ones and obtain, according to the effectivity required, Martin-Löf randomness and the weaker notions of computable, Schnorr and Kurtz randomness.

We use the notation of the course on Martin-Löf randomness: $[\sigma]$ is the cylinder of the sequences extending $\sigma$, $[W]^{\prec}$ the open class generated by a set of strings $W$, and $\lambda$ the uniform measure. The empty string is written $\varepsilon$; small positive reals are written $\delta$.

## Betting strategies

A gambler bets on the bits of an unknown sequence $X \in \cs$, revealed one at a time. She starts with a capital $d(\varepsilon) \geq 0$. When she has seen the prefix $\sigma$ and holds the capital $d(\sigma)$, she chooses a bit $i$ and a stake $s$ with $0 \leq s \leq d(\sigma)$; if the next bit is $i$ she wins $s$, otherwise she loses it. So $d(\sigma i) = d(\sigma) + s$ and $d(\sigma (1-i)) = d(\sigma) - s$, and in both cases
$$
d(\sigma 0) + d(\sigma 1) = 2 d(\sigma).
$$
The game is fair: the average of the capital after the bet, over the two outcomes, equals the capital before it. The bound $s \leq d(\sigma)$ means that the gambler cannot borrow, so her capital stays non-negative. Conversely, every non-negative function satisfying this equality describes such a strategy: at $\sigma$, she stakes $|d(\sigma 0) - d(\sigma)|$ on the bit whose branch has the larger value.

:::definition[Ville, Lévy]{#def-martingale}
A **martingale** is a function $d : \bstr \to [0, \infty)$ such that $d(\sigma 0) + d(\sigma 1) = 2 d(\sigma)$ for every string $\sigma$. A **supermartingale** is a function $d : \bstr \to [0, \infty)$ such that $d(\sigma 0) + d(\sigma 1) \leq 2 d(\sigma)$ for every $\sigma$. A (super)martingale $d$ **succeeds** on $X \in \cs$ if $\sup_n d(X \uh n) = \infty$. The **success set** $S[d]$ is the class of sequences on which $d$ succeeds.
:::

A supermartingale is a gambler who may also throw part of her capital away before betting. Since every value $d(X \uh n)$ is finite, $\sup_n d(X \uh n) = \infty$ if and only if $\limsup_n d(X \uh n) = \infty$: success only requires the capital to be unbounded, and the capital may fall close to $0$ infinitely often. The class $S[d] = \bigcap_k \bigcup_n \{ X : d(X \uh n) > k \}$ is an intersection of open classes, hence a Borel class.

:::example{#ex-martingales}
1. A constant function is a martingale: the gambler never bets. It succeeds on no sequence.
2. For a string $\tau$, the **elementary martingale** $E_\tau$ bets its whole capital on the bits of $\tau$ until $\tau$ is exhausted, and then stops betting. With initial capital $1$: $E_\tau(\sigma) = 2^{|\sigma|}$ if $\sigma \preceq \tau$, $E_\tau(\sigma) = 2^{|\tau|}$ if $\tau \preceq \sigma$, and $E_\tau(\sigma) = 0$ if $\sigma$ and $\tau$ are incomparable. It is bounded by $2^{|\tau|}$.
3. For a Borel class $\mathcal C$, let $d_{\mathcal C}(\sigma) = 2^{|\sigma|} \lambda(\mathcal C \cap [\sigma])$, the probability of landing in $\mathcal C$ when the first bits are $\sigma$. Since $[\sigma]$ is the disjoint union of $[\sigma 0]$ and $[\sigma 1]$, additivity gives the martingale equality. Its initial capital is $\lambda(\mathcal C)$, it is bounded by $1$, and $d_{\mathcal C}(\sigma) = 1$ whenever $[\sigma] \subseteq \mathcal C$. The elementary martingale is $E_\tau = 2^{|\tau|} d_{[\tau]}$.
4. Fix $\delta \in [0, 1]$. The **proportional strategy** $p_\delta$ bets the fraction $\delta$ of its capital on the bit $1$ at every step: $p_\delta(\varepsilon) = 1$, $p_\delta(\sigma 1) = (1 + \delta) p_\delta(\sigma)$ and $p_\delta(\sigma 0) = (1 - \delta) p_\delta(\sigma)$. So $p_\delta(\sigma) = (1 + \delta)^{k} (1 - \delta)^{|\sigma| - k}$, where $k$ is the number of ones in $\sigma$. :ref{#fig-betting-tree} shows $p_{1/3}$ on the strings of length at most $3$.
:::

The proportional strategy wins on sequences with too many ones. With $\delta = \frac13$, if $X \uh n$ has $k \geq \frac23 n$ ones, then, since $p_{1/3}(\sigma)$ increases with the number of ones,
$$
p_{1/3}(X \uh n) \geq \left(\tfrac43\right)^{2n/3} \left(\tfrac23\right)^{n/3} = \left(\tfrac{32}{27}\right)^{n/3}.
$$
So $p_{1/3}$ succeeds on every sequence whose prefixes have a frequency of ones at least $\frac23$ at infinitely many lengths. On a sequence whose frequency of ones $k_n/n$ in $X \uh n$ tends to $\frac12$, on the contrary, $p_{1/3}(X \uh n) = \big((\frac43)^{k_n/n} (\frac23)^{1 - k_n/n}\big)^n$, and the base tends to $(\frac89)^{1/2} < 1$, so the capital tends to $0$: a strategy that bets on a regularity loses when the regularity is absent. The lesson on computable randomness treats all deviations from the law of large numbers with these strategies.

## Basic properties

:::lemma{#lem-martingale-basic}
Let $d$ be a supermartingale.

1. $d(\sigma\tau) \leq 2^{|\tau|} d(\sigma)$ for all strings $\sigma, \tau$; in particular $d(\sigma) \leq 2^{|\sigma|} d(\varepsilon)$. If $d(\sigma) = 0$, then $d(\sigma\tau) = 0$ for every $\tau$.
2. If $d_0, d_1, \dots$ are (super)martingales with $\sum_n d_n(\varepsilon) < \infty$, then $\sum_n d_n$ is a (super)martingale. A product $c\, d$ with $c \geq 0$ is a (super)martingale.
3. There is a martingale $d' \geq d$ with $d'(\varepsilon) = d(\varepsilon)$.
:::

:::proof{of=lem-martingale-basic}
1. Since the values are non-negative, $d(\sigma i) \leq d(\sigma 0) + d(\sigma 1) \leq 2 d(\sigma)$ for $i \in \{0, 1\}$; iterate along $\tau$. If $d(\sigma) = 0$, both children have value $0$, and so on.
2. By (1), $\sum_n d_n(\sigma) \leq 2^{|\sigma|} \sum_n d_n(\varepsilon)$ is finite for every $\sigma$; the (in)equality passes to sums of convergent series. The second statement is clear.
3. Let $r(\sigma) = d(\sigma) - \frac12(d(\sigma 0) + d(\sigma 1)) \geq 0$, the amount thrown away at $\sigma$, and $d'(\sigma) = d(\sigma) + \sum_{\rho \prec \sigma} r(\rho)$, where $\rho$ ranges over the proper prefixes of $\sigma$. Then $d' \geq d \geq 0$, $d'(\varepsilon) = d(\varepsilon)$, and
$$
\tfrac12 (d'(\sigma 0) + d'(\sigma 1)) = \tfrac12 (d(\sigma 0) + d(\sigma 1)) + r(\sigma) + \sum_{\rho \prec \sigma} r(\rho) = d'(\sigma).
$$
:::

Every supermartingale fails somewhere: at each node, one of the two children has a value at most that of the node, and following such children gives a sequence along which the capital never increases.

:::lemma{#lem-non-ascending}
Let $d$ be a supermartingale, and define $X$ by $X(n) = 0$ if $d((X \uh n) 0) \leq d(X \uh n)$, and $X(n) = 1$ otherwise. Then $d(X \uh (n+1)) \leq d(X \uh n)$ for every $n$, so $d(X \uh n) \leq d(\varepsilon)$ for every $n$. The sequence $X$ is the **leftmost non-ascending path** of $d$.
:::

:::proof{of=lem-non-ascending}
If $d((X \uh n) 0) > d(X \uh n)$, then $d((X \uh n) 1) \leq 2 d(X \uh n) - d((X \uh n) 0) < d(X \uh n)$.
:::

## Kolmogorov's inequality

A gambler who starts with capital $1$ cannot reach capital $b$ on more than a fraction $1/b$ of the sequences: each time she wins on some sequences, she has lost on others. The following inequality, due to Ville, makes this precise; it is usually called Kolmogorov's inequality, by analogy with Kolmogorov's maximal inequality for sums of independent random variables.

:::lemma[Kolmogorov's inequality; Ville]{#lem-kolmogorov-inequality}
Let $d$ be a supermartingale.

1. For every string $\sigma$ and every prefix-free set $S$ of extensions of $\sigma$,
$$
\sum_{\tau \in S} 2^{-|\tau|} d(\tau) \leq 2^{-|\sigma|} d(\sigma).
$$
2. For every real $b > 0$, $\lambda(\{ X : \exists n\ d(X \uh n) \geq b \}) \leq d(\varepsilon)/b$.
:::

:::proof{of=lem-kolmogorov-inequality}
1. An infinite sum of non-negative terms is the supremum of its finite partial sums, and every subset of $S$ is prefix-free; so we may assume $S$ finite and non-empty. We argue by induction on $L(S) = \max_{\tau \in S} |\tau| - |\sigma|$. If $\sigma \in S$, then $S = \{ \sigma \}$, since $S$ is prefix-free and all its elements extend $\sigma$; this is the case $L(S) = 0$, and equality holds. Otherwise, every element of $S$ extends $\sigma 0$ or $\sigma 1$. Let $S_i$ be the set of elements of $S$ extending $\sigma i$: it is a prefix-free set of extensions of $\sigma i$, and if it is non-empty, $L(S_i) = L(S) - 1$ when computed from $\sigma i$, or less. By the induction hypothesis, and since an empty $S_i$ contributes $0$,
$$
\sum_{\tau \in S} 2^{-|\tau|} d(\tau) \leq 2^{-|\sigma| - 1} \big(d(\sigma 0) + d(\sigma 1)\big) \leq 2^{-|\sigma|} d(\sigma).
$$
2. The class is $[R]^{\prec}$ with $R = \{ \tau : d(\tau) \geq b \}$. Let $S$ be the set of the elements of $R$ with no proper prefix in $R$: it is prefix-free and generates the same open class. By additivity and (1) with $\sigma = \varepsilon$,
$$
\lambda([S]^{\prec}) = \sum_{\tau \in S} 2^{-|\tau|} \leq \sum_{\tau \in S} 2^{-|\tau|} \frac{d(\tau)}{b} \leq \frac{d(\varepsilon)}{b}.
$$
:::

For a martingale, the weighted sum $\sum_{|\tau| = n} 2^{-n} d(\tau)$ over all strings of length $n$ equals $d(\varepsilon)$: it is the average capital after $n$ bets. Part (1) says that stopping at a prefix-free set of times, chosen by looking at the bits seen so far, does not increase this average. The measure-theoretic content of the lemma is that a capital above $b$ is a rare event, of probability at most $d(\varepsilon)/b$; this is Markov's inequality, applied to the capital at a stopping time.

:::quiz[Kolmogorov's inequality]
In the proof of :ref{#lem-kolmogorov-inequality} (1), where is the supermartingale inequality used?

- [x] In the inductive step, to replace the contributions of the two subtrees above $\sigma 0$ and $\sigma 1$ by $2^{-|\sigma|} d(\sigma)$.
- [ ] In the base case $S = \{ \sigma \}$.

  The base case is an equality and uses nothing.
- [ ] To show that the sets $S_i$ are prefix-free.

  Subsets of a prefix-free set are prefix-free; no property of $d$ is involved.

---

In (2), why does the proof pass from $R$ to the set $S$ of its minimal elements?

- [x] Because the inequality of (1) holds only for prefix-free sets, and the minimal elements form a prefix-free set generating the same open class.
- [ ] Because $R$ may be infinite.

  (1) applies to infinite prefix-free sets; the issue is that $R$ is not prefix-free: an extension of a string with $d \geq b$ may also have $d \geq b$.
- [ ] Because the measure of $[R]^{\prec}$ is not defined.

  Open classes are Borel, so they have a measure.
:::

## Exercises

::::exercise{difficulty=1}
Let $\mathcal N$ be the class of the sequences $X$ with $X(2i) = X(2i+1)$ for every $i$ (:ref{#ex-null-classes} (1)). Define explicitly a martingale $d$ with $d(\varepsilon) = 1$ such that $d(X \uh 2n) = 2^n$ for every $X \in \mathcal N$ and every $n$.

:::solution
At a string $\sigma$ of even length, bet nothing: $d(\sigma 0) = d(\sigma 1) = d(\sigma)$. At a string $\sigma = \rho i$ of odd length, bet the whole capital on the bit $i$: $d(\rho i i) = 2 d(\rho i)$ and $d(\rho i (1-i)) = 0$. The martingale equality holds at every string, and along a member of $\mathcal N$ every bet at an odd length is won, so the capital doubles at each pair of bits.
:::
::::

::::exercise{difficulty=1}
Show that the martingales $d$ with $d(\varepsilon) = 1$ are exactly the functions $\sigma \mapsto 2^{|\sigma|} \mu(\sigma)$, where $\mu : \bstr \to [0, 1]$ satisfies $\mu(\varepsilon) = 1$ and $\mu(\sigma) = \mu(\sigma 0) + \mu(\sigma 1)$ for every $\sigma$. Which function $\mu$ corresponds to the constant martingale $1$, and which to $E_\tau$?

:::solution
If $d$ is a martingale with $d(\varepsilon) = 1$, let $\mu(\sigma) = 2^{-|\sigma|} d(\sigma)$. Then $\mu(\sigma 0) + \mu(\sigma 1) = 2^{-|\sigma| - 1}(d(\sigma 0) + d(\sigma 1)) = 2^{-|\sigma|} d(\sigma) = \mu(\sigma)$, and $\mu(\varepsilon) = 1$. By induction on $n$, the values of $\mu$ on the strings of length $n$ sum to $\mu(\varepsilon) = 1$; they are non-negative, so each is at most $1$. The converse computation is the same. The constant martingale $1$ corresponds to $\mu(\sigma) = 2^{-|\sigma|}$, the uniform measure of the cylinders, and $E_\tau$ to the measure concentrated on $[\tau]$ and uniform inside it: $\mu(\sigma) = 2^{-|\sigma|} E_\tau(\sigma)$, which is $1$ for $\sigma \preceq \tau$ and $2^{|\tau| - |\sigma|}$ for $\sigma \succeq \tau$. (By Carathéodory's theorem, such functions $\mu$ are the probability measures on $\cs$, through $\mu([\sigma]) = \mu(\sigma)$.)
:::
::::

::::exercise{difficulty=2}
Let $d$ be a supermartingale with $d(\varepsilon) = 1$, and let $b > 1$. Show that the class $\mathcal P_b = \{ X : \forall n\ d(X \uh n) < b \}$ is closed and has measure at least $1 - 1/b$. Give, for each integer $k \geq 1$, a martingale for which $\lambda(\mathcal P_{2^k}) = 1 - 2^{-k}$.

:::solution
The complement of $\mathcal P_b$ is the open class $\{ X : \exists n\ d(X \uh n) \geq b \}$, of measure at most $1/b$ by :ref{#lem-kolmogorov-inequality} (2). For the example, take $d = E_{0^k}$: its capital reaches $2^k$ exactly on the sequences extending $0^k$, and stays below $2^k$ elsewhere, so $\mathcal P_{2^k} = \cs \setminus [0^k]$ has measure $1 - 2^{-k}$.
:::
::::

## Notes and references

Martingales were introduced into the foundations of probability by Ville (1939); Lévy had studied the corresponding sequences of random variables. The martingales of this course, functions on strings, are a special case of the martingales of probability theory, where the fairness condition is conditioned on the past values of the capital rather than on the past bits (Downey–Hirschfeldt, Section 6.3.4). Downey and Hirschfeldt present martingales, supermartingales and Kolmogorov's inequality in Section 6.3.1 (Definition 6.3.1, Proposition 6.3.2, Theorem 6.3.3); Nies treats the same material in Section 7.1 (Definition 7.1.1, Proposition 7.1.6 for :ref{#lem-martingale-basic} (3), Fact 7.1.7, Lemma 7.1.8 and Proposition 7.1.9 for Kolmogorov's inequality, Fact 7.1.12 for the leftmost non-ascending path). The examples $E_\tau$ and $d_{\mathcal C}$ follow Nies (Examples 7.1.3 and 7.1.4), and the second exercise is his Fact 7.1.2. Monin and Patey mention the martingale approach in a remark of their chapter on Martin-Löf randomness.

- R. G. Downey and D. R. Hirschfeldt, *Algorithmic Randomness and Complexity*, Springer, 2010, Section 6.3.
- A. Nies, *Computability and Randomness*, Oxford University Press, 2009, Section 7.1.
