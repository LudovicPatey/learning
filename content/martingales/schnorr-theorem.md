Ville's theorem allows every null class, so every sequence is defeated by some martingale: the one built from the covers of the singleton. As with tests, randomness requires a restriction to effective strategies. The proof of Ville's theorem translates covers into strategies and back; Schnorr (1971) observed that both translations are effective when the covers form a Martin-Löf test and the strategies are approximable from below. This lesson proves Schnorr's theorem and constructs universal and optimal effective strategies.

## Effective martingales

A real number is left-c.e. if it is the limit of a computable non-decreasing sequence of rationals (:ref{to="martin-lof-randomness/measure"}). Effectivity of a martingale is defined through its values.

:::definition{#def-ce-martingale}
A (super)martingale $d$ is **computably enumerable**, or **c.e.**, if its values are left-c.e. uniformly: there is a computable function $(\sigma, s) \mapsto d_s(\sigma) \in \mathbb Q_{\geq 0}$, non-decreasing in $s$, with $d(\sigma) = \lim_s d_s(\sigma)$ for every $\sigma$. It is **computable** if there is a computable function $(\sigma, n) \mapsto q(\sigma, n) \in \mathbb Q$ with $|d(\sigma) - q(\sigma, n)| \leq 2^{-n}$ for all $\sigma$ and $n$.
:::

The functions $d_s$ are not required to be martingales. A computable martingale is c.e.: the rationals $q(\sigma, n) - 2^{-n}$ approximate $d(\sigma)$ from below, and their running maximum is non-decreasing. For a c.e. martingale and a rational $q$, the relation $d(\sigma) > q$ is $\Sigma^0_1$, uniformly in $\sigma$ and $q$: it holds if and only if $d_s(\sigma) > q$ for some $s$. The relation $d(\sigma) \geq q$ is not $\Sigma^0_1$ in general.

A c.e. martingale models a gambler who does not know her capital: she learns better and better lower bounds on it, and so, since $d(\sigma 0) - d(\sigma)$ is the difference of two left-c.e. reals, she does not know her bets either. Schnorr considered this the natural effective counterpart of Martin-Löf tests, whose measures are known only from below; the next lesson studies the strategies whose bets are known.

:::example{#ex-ce-martingales}
1. The strategies $E_\tau$ and $p_\delta$ for a rational $\delta \in [0, 1]$ of :ref{#ex-martingales} are computable, with rational values computable from $\sigma$ (and $\tau$, $\delta$).
2. Let $\mathcal U = [W]^{\prec}$ with $W$ c.e. Then $d_{\mathcal U}(\sigma) = 2^{|\sigma|} \lambda(\mathcal U \cap [\sigma])$ is a c.e. martingale, uniformly in an index of $W$. Indeed $\mathcal U \cap [\sigma]$ is generated by the c.e. set of strings $\sigma\rho$ such that some prefix of $\sigma\rho$ is in $W$, so its measure is left-c.e., uniformly in $\sigma$, by :ref{#prop-measure-effective} (1). In general $d_{\mathcal U}$ is not computable: $d_{\mathcal U}(\varepsilon) = \lambda(\mathcal U)$, which can be any left-c.e. real of $[0, 1]$, for instance $\Omega$.
:::

:::lemma{#lem-ce-sum}
If $(d_n)$ is a sequence of c.e. (super)martingales, uniformly in $n$, with $\sum_n d_n(\varepsilon) < \infty$, then $d = \sum_n d_n$ is a c.e. (super)martingale.
:::

:::proof{of=lem-ce-sum}
By :ref{#lem-martingale-basic} (2), $d$ is a (super)martingale. Let $(d_{n,s})$ be the uniform approximations, and let $D_s(\sigma) = \sum_{n \leq s} d_{n,s}(\sigma)$, a computable function, non-decreasing in $s$. Each $D_s(\sigma)$ is at most $d(\sigma)$, and for every $N$, $\lim_s D_s(\sigma) \geq \sum_{n \leq N} d_n(\sigma)$; so $\lim_s D_s(\sigma) = d(\sigma)$.
:::

## Schnorr's theorem

The proof of Ville's theorem gives the two translations. From a strategy, the classes where the capital exceeds $2^k$ are covers of measure at most $2^{-k}$ by Kolmogorov's inequality; for a c.e. strategy, they are $\Sigma^0_1$, uniformly in $k$, provided the threshold is written with a strict inequality. From a Martin-Löf test $(\mathcal U_n)$, the sum of the martingales $d_{\mathcal U_n}$ is c.e. by the example and :ref{#lem-ce-sum}.

:::theorem[Schnorr]{#thm-schnorr-martingales}
For a sequence $X$, the following are equivalent.

1. $X$ is Martin-Löf random.
2. No c.e. supermartingale succeeds on $X$.
3. No c.e. martingale succeeds on $X$.
:::

:::proof{of=thm-schnorr-martingales}
(2) ⇒ (3) holds since a martingale is a supermartingale.

(3) ⇒ (1). Suppose that $X$ is captured by a Martin-Löf test $(\mathcal U_n)$. The martingales $d_{\mathcal U_n}$ are c.e., uniformly in $n$, by :ref{#ex-ce-martingales} (2), and $\sum_n d_{\mathcal U_n}(\varepsilon) = \sum_n \lambda(\mathcal U_n) \leq 2$. By :ref{#lem-ce-sum}, $d = \sum_n d_{\mathcal U_n}$ is a c.e. martingale. As in the proof of :ref{#thm-ville}, $X$ belongs to every $\mathcal U_n$, so $\lim_m d(X \uh m) = \infty$.

(1) ⇒ (2). Let $d$ be a c.e. supermartingale that succeeds on $X$. Then $d(\varepsilon) > 0$, since otherwise $d = 0$. Let $c$ be a positive integer with $d(\varepsilon) \leq c$; the supermartingale $d/c$ is c.e., with approximations $d_s/c$, and succeeds on $X$. Let
$$
\mathcal U_k = [\{ \sigma : d(\sigma)/c > 2^k \}]^{\prec}.
$$
The set of strings is c.e., uniformly in $k$, so $(\mathcal U_k)$ is uniformly $\Sigma^0_1$. By :ref{#lem-kolmogorov-inequality} (2), $\lambda(\mathcal U_k) \leq \lambda(\{ Y : \exists n\ d(Y \uh n)/c \geq 2^k \}) \leq 2^{-k}$. So $(\mathcal U_k)$ is a Martin-Löf test. Since $d/c$ is unbounded along $X$, $X$ belongs to every $\mathcal U_k$, and $X$ is not random.
:::

:::quiz[Schnorr's theorem]
In the proof of (1) ⇒ (2), why is $\mathcal U_k$ defined with $d(\sigma)/c > 2^k$ rather than $d(\sigma)/c \geq 2^k$?

- [x] Because for a left-c.e. real $r$, the relation $r > 2^k$ is $\Sigma^0_1$, witnessed by an approximation above $2^k$, while $r \geq 2^k$ need not be.
- [ ] Because Kolmogorov's inequality applies only to strict inequalities.

  :ref{#lem-kolmogorov-inequality} (2) is stated with $\geq b$; the class with $>$ is smaller, so the bound holds a fortiori.
- [ ] Because $d(\sigma)/c$ is never equal to $2^k$.

  It may be equal; the choice of $>$ is about the complexity of the relation.

---

Where is the integer $c$ used, and why need it not be computed from $d$?

- [x] It normalizes the initial capital to at most $1$, so that the measures are at most $2^{-k}$; a single number is fixed in advance, and the test is uniformly $\Sigma^0_1$ for this fixed $c$.
- [ ] It makes $d/c$ computable.

  Dividing by $c$ does not change the complexity of $d$: $d/c$ is c.e.
- [ ] It is needed for $d/c$ to be a supermartingale.

  Any positive multiple of a supermartingale is a supermartingale.
:::

Schnorr's theorem completes the equivalence of the three approaches: by the Levin–Schnorr theorem (:ref{#thm-levin-schnorr}), a sequence is Martin-Löf random if and only if its prefixes are incompressible, if and only if it avoids every effectively null class, if and only if no c.e. betting strategy wins on it. Applied to a universal test, the proof gives a single strategy.

:::corollary[Schnorr]{#cor-universal-ce-martingale}
There is a c.e. martingale $d$ with $d(\varepsilon) \leq 2$ such that $\lim_m d(X \uh m) = \infty$ for every sequence $X$ that is not Martin-Löf random, and $\sup_m d(X \uh m) < \infty$ for every Martin-Löf random $X$.
:::

:::proof{of=cor-universal-ce-martingale}
Apply the proof of (3) ⇒ (1) to a universal Martin-Löf test (:ref{#thm-universal-test}): every non-random $X$ is captured by it. The martingale obtained is c.e., so it does not succeed on random sequences.
:::

The savings trick (:ref{#prop-savings}) is not effective for c.e. martingales: the $k$-th copy must stop betting when the capital of $d$ exceeds $2^k$, and for a c.e. martingale this is known only in the limit, with values only approximated. The corollary shows that the conclusion of the trick still holds: the success set of a c.e. martingale is the class captured by the test of the proof of (1) ⇒ (2), and the c.e. martingale built from this test has capital tending to infinity on it.

## Optimal supermartingales

The universal martingale of the corollary succeeds on every non-random sequence, but it need not be large wherever another strategy is large. A stronger form of universality is available for supermartingales: a single c.e. supermartingale that dominates every c.e. supermartingale up to a multiplicative constant. Its construction follows the pattern of the universal test: list all candidates, trim each into a genuine supermartingale without changing those that already are, and add them with weights.

:::theorem[Schnorr; Levin]{#thm-optimal-supermartingale}
There is a c.e. supermartingale $D$ that is **optimal**: for every c.e. supermartingale $d$ there is a constant $c$ with $d(\sigma) \leq c\, D(\sigma)$ for every $\sigma$.
:::

:::proof{of=thm-optimal-supermartingale}
Let $(W_e)$ be the standard enumeration of the c.e. subsets of $\bstr \times \mathbb Q_{\geq 0}$, and for each $e$ let $f_s(\sigma)$ be the largest $q$ with $(\sigma, q) \in W_{e,s}$, or $0$ if there is none (we omit the index $e$). We define rationals $L_s(\sigma)$, computable from $e$, $s$ and $\sigma$, by recursion on $s$ and, inside a stage, on the length of $\sigma$. Let $L_0 = 0$. At stage $s + 1$, let $L_{s+1}(\varepsilon) = \max(L_s(\varepsilon), \min(f_{s+1}(\varepsilon), 1))$, and, once $L_{s+1}(\sigma)$ is defined,
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{s+1}(\sigma 0) &= \max\big(L_s(\sigma 0),\ \min(f_{s+1}(\sigma 0),\ 2 L_{s+1}(\sigma) - L_s(\sigma 1))\big), \\
L_{s+1}(\sigma 1) &= \max\big(L_s(\sigma 1),\ \min(f_{s+1}(\sigma 1),\ 2 L_{s+1}(\sigma) - L_{s+1}(\sigma 0))\big).
\end{aligned}
$$
The values never decrease, and each child may grow only into the room left by its sibling. We check by induction on $s$ that $L_s$ is a supermartingale with $L_s(\varepsilon) \leq 1$ and $L_s \leq f_s$. This is clear for $s = 0$. At stage $s + 1$, suppose $L_{s+1}(\sigma) \geq L_s(\sigma)$. If $L_{s+1}(\sigma 0) = L_s(\sigma 0)$, then $L_{s+1}(\sigma 0) + L_s(\sigma 1) \leq 2 L_s(\sigma) \leq 2 L_{s+1}(\sigma)$; otherwise $L_{s+1}(\sigma 0) \leq 2 L_{s+1}(\sigma) - L_s(\sigma 1)$. In both cases $L_{s+1}(\sigma 0) + L_s(\sigma 1) \leq 2 L_{s+1}(\sigma)$, and the same case distinction for $\sigma 1$ gives $L_{s+1}(\sigma 0) + L_{s+1}(\sigma 1) \leq 2 L_{s+1}(\sigma)$. The bounds $L_{s+1} \leq f_{s+1}$ and $L_{s+1}(\varepsilon) \leq 1$ hold since $f_s \leq f_{s+1}$.

Let $L^e(\sigma) = \lim_s L_s(\sigma)$. It is a c.e. supermartingale with $L^e(\varepsilon) \leq 1$, uniformly in $e$, and $L^e \leq f$, where $f(\sigma) = \sup_s f_s(\sigma)$. Suppose now that $f$ is a supermartingale with finite values and $f(\varepsilon) \leq 1$. We show $L^e = f$ by induction on the length. At the root, $L_s(\varepsilon) \geq \min(f_s(\varepsilon), 1) = f_s(\varepsilon)$, which tends to $f(\varepsilon)$. If $L^e(\sigma) = f(\sigma)$, then, since $L_s(\sigma 1) \leq f(\sigma 1)$,
$$
L_{s+1}(\sigma 0) \geq \min\big(f_{s+1}(\sigma 0),\ 2 L_{s+1}(\sigma) - f(\sigma 1)\big).
$$
As $s$ tends to infinity, the first term tends to $f(\sigma 0)$ and the second to $2 f(\sigma) - f(\sigma 1) \geq f(\sigma 0)$; so $L^e(\sigma 0) \geq f(\sigma 0)$, hence $L^e(\sigma 0) = f(\sigma 0)$. The same argument applies to $\sigma 1$, using $L_{s+1}(\sigma 0) \leq f(\sigma 0)$.

Every c.e. supermartingale $d$ with $d(\varepsilon) \leq 1$ is one of the $L^e$: take $e$ with $W_e = \{ (\sigma, q) : q < d(\sigma) \}$, a c.e. set, for which $f = d$. Let $D = \sum_e 2^{-e-1} L^e$, a c.e. supermartingale by :ref{#lem-ce-sum}. If $d$ is a c.e. supermartingale and $c \geq d(\varepsilon)$ is a positive integer, then $d/c = L^e$ for some $e$, and $d \leq c\, 2^{e+1} D$.
:::

By :ref{#thm-schnorr-martingales}, an optimal supermartingale $D$ is bounded along every random sequence and unbounded along every non-random one. For martingales, the theorem fails: there is no optimal c.e. martingale (Downey, Griffiths and LaForte 2004; Downey–Hirschfeldt, Theorem 6.3.12), a fact admitted here. The proof above does not adapt: trimming produces supermartingales, and the martingale above a c.e. supermartingale given by :ref{#lem-martingale-basic} (3) need not be c.e., since the amounts $L(\sigma) - \frac12(L(\sigma 0) + L(\sigma 1))$ that it adds are differences of left-c.e. reals. Levin's optimal semimeasure, through which the a priori complexity $KM$ is defined, is the same object: $\sigma \mapsto 2^{-|\sigma|} D(\sigma)$ (Downey–Hirschfeldt, Section 6.3.2).

## Exercises

::::exercise{difficulty=2}
For a string $\tau$, let $E_\tau$ be the elementary martingale of :ref{#ex-martingales}, and let $d_K = \sum_\tau 2^{-K(\tau)} E_\tau$. Show that $d_K$ is a c.e. martingale and that $d_K(X \uh n) \geq \sum_{m \leq n} 2^{m - K(X \uh m)}$ for every $X$ and $n$. Deduce from :ref{#thm-schnorr-martingales} the direction ($\Rightarrow$) of the ample excess lemma (:ref{#thm-ample-excess}): if $X$ is random, then $\sum_n 2^{n - K(X \uh n)} < \infty$.

:::solution
Since $E_\tau(\varepsilon) = 1$, $\sum_\tau 2^{-K(\tau)} E_\tau(\varepsilon) = \sum_\tau 2^{-K(\tau)} \leq 1$ by :ref{#prop-k-basic} (4), so $d_K$ is a martingale by :ref{#lem-martingale-basic} (2). The function $K$ is approximable from above, so $2^{-K(\tau)}$ is left-c.e. uniformly in $\tau$, and $2^{-K(\tau)} E_\tau$ is a c.e. martingale, uniformly in $\tau$ (the values of $E_\tau$ are computable). By :ref{#lem-ce-sum}, $d_K$ is c.e. For $m \leq n$ and $\tau = X \uh m$, $E_\tau(X \uh n) = 2^m$, so the terms indexed by the prefixes of $X \uh n$ contribute $\sum_{m \leq n} 2^{m - K(X \uh m)}$. If $X$ is random, $d_K$ is bounded along $X$ by the theorem, and the partial sums of the series are bounded.
:::
::::

::::exercise{#ex-partial-martingale difficulty=2}
A **partial computable martingale** is a partial computable function $d$ from strings to non-negative rationals whose domain is closed under prefixes, such that for every $\sigma$, $d(\sigma 0)$ is defined if and only if $d(\sigma 1)$ is, and then $d(\sigma 0) + d(\sigma 1) = 2 d(\sigma)$. It succeeds on $X$ if $d(X \uh n)$ is defined for every $n$ and unbounded. A sequence is **partial computably random** if no partial computable martingale succeeds on it. Show that every Martin-Löf random sequence is partial computably random.

:::solution
Let $\tilde d(\sigma) = d(\sigma)$ if $d(\sigma)$ is defined, and $\tilde d(\sigma) = 0$ otherwise. It is c.e.: the approximation at stage $s$ is $0$ until the computation of $d(\sigma)$ halts, and $d(\sigma)$ afterwards. It is a supermartingale: if $d(\sigma 0)$ and $d(\sigma 1)$ are defined, so is $d(\sigma)$ and equality holds; if they are undefined, $\tilde d(\sigma 0) + \tilde d(\sigma 1) = 0$. If $d$ succeeds on $X$, so does $\tilde d$, and $X$ is not random by :ref{#thm-schnorr-martingales}.
:::
::::

## Notes and references

Schnorr (1971) proved :ref{#thm-schnorr-martingales}, the existence of a universal c.e. martingale and of an optimal c.e. supermartingale, in his monograph and in *A unified approach to the definition of a random sequence*; Levin's optimal semimeasure (Zvonkin and Levin 1970) is essentially the same as the optimal supermartingale. The absence of an optimal c.e. martingale was first proved in print by Downey, Griffiths and LaForte (2004), though it is implicit in Levin's work. Downey and Hirschfeldt present these results in Section 6.3 (Theorem 6.3.4, Corollary 6.3.5, Theorem 6.3.7, Theorem 6.3.12); Nies in Section 7.2 (Definition 7.2.1, Proposition 7.2.6, Exercise 7.2.7). The martingale $d_K$ of the first exercise and its relation with the ample excess lemma are from Nies (Theorem 7.2.8); partial computable martingales are Nies, Definition 7.4.1.
